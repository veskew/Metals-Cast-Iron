03 · Environmental impact

Environmental Impacts

Cast Iron is 100% recyclable. It can be melted down and recast into new shapes.

Scrapyards will pay around $295 a ton for cast iron [4], which translates to around $2–3 per individual brake rotor. Typically, mechanics keep the old brake rotors and throw them into a scrap bin when replacing. Over time, the mechanics amass a significant amount of cast iron that they can bring to a scrapyard and make some money off of. Typically mechanics charge between 500 to 1,000 dollars for replacement of brake rotors [5], and they get to keep 8–12 dollars of value from the four brake rotors in a car.

Mechanics are in the best position to recycle cast iron, as they can easily bring big deposits to the scrapyard in bulk. Typically, people are okay leaving their brake rotors with their mechanic, and mechanics are okay bringing large deposits to the scrapyard. If people need more incentives to leave their rotors with the mechanic, they could start offering 1–2% cash back on the replacement work if they agree to leave their brake rotor. If the mechanics need more incentives to get the scrap to the scrapyard, the scrapyard owners could do scheduled pickups of scrap from mechanics, saving the mechanics time and money. We could further incentivize this by having mechanics offer a 1% cash back on their purchase if they let their old scrap stay with the mechanic.

It is encouraging to see products like brake rotors.

They serve an important function in automobile safety (the ability for a car to brake is perhaps the most effective safety feature a car can have). They also are made entirely of one material, and that one material is easy to recycle. When we compared an XRF reading of the brake rotor with an XRF reading of an ingot made by casting pieces of the brake rotor (Table 1 and Table 2), the material was still mostly iron (95.8% before and 95.4% after), but the composition did shift: silicon dropped from 3.05% to 2.50%, and sulfur rose from 0.19% to 1.04%. Recasting keeps the material recognizably gray cast iron, but its chemistry should be checked and adjusted before the recycled iron is put back into a part as demanding as a brake rotor.

Pollution from brake rotors in use

A brake rotor pollutes long before it reaches the scrapyard. Every time a driver brakes, the pad grinds against the rotor, and both wear away into fine dust. The OECD notes that most disc brakes are made of pearlitic gray cast iron, the same material we cast, and that pads and discs contribute almost equally to brake wear particles [6].

These particles matter for health. Airborne particulate matter is linked to respiratory infections, lung cancer, and chronic respiratory and cardiovascular disease [6]. Exhaust standards have cut tailpipe particles for decades, but nothing regulated wear particles. The OECD projects that non-exhaust particles from passenger vehicles will rise 53.5% by 2030, and that they could make up most road-traffic particulate matter by 2035 [6].

The EU's Euro 7 regulation, adopted in April 2024, is the first vehicle standard to put a legal limit on brake dust [7]. For new types of cars and vans from November 29, 2026, brake particle emissions are capped at 7 mg/km per vehicle for combustion and hybrid vehicles and 3 mg/km for pure electric vehicles until the end of 2029 [7]. From 2035, the limit drops to 3 mg/km for all cars and vans [7].

A rotor's environmental footprint is not just its recyclability at end of life, but also what it sheds onto our roads and into our lungs while in use.
