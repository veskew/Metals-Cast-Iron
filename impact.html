Environmental Impacts

Cast Iron is 100% recyclable. It can be melted down and recast into new shapes.

Scrapyards will pay around $295 a ton for cast iron [4], which translates to around $2–3 per individual brake rotor. Typically, mechanics keep the old brake rotors and throw them into a scrap bin when replacing. Over time, the mechanics amass a significant amount of cast iron that they can bring to a scrapyard and make some money off of. Typically mechanics charge between 500 to 1,000 dollars for replacement of brake rotors [5], and they get to keep 8–12 dollars of value from the four brake rotors in a car.

Mechanics are in the best position to recycle cast iron, as they can easily bring big deposits to the scrapyard in bulk. Typically, people are okay leaving their brake rotors with their mechanic, and mechanics are okay bringing large deposits to the scrapyard. If people need more incentives to leave their rotors with the mechanic, they could start offering 1–2% cash back on the replacement work if they agree to leave their brake rotor. If the mechanics need more incentives to get the scrap to the scrapyard, the scrapyard owners could do scheduled pickups of scrap from mechanics, saving the mechanics time and money. We could further incentivize this by having mechanics offer a 1% cash back on their purchase if they let their old scrap stay with the mechanic.

It is encouraging to see products like brake rotors.

They serve an important function in automobile safety (the ability for a car to brake is perhaps the most effective safety feature a car can have). They also are made entirely of one material, and that one material is easy to recycle. As evidenced by us taking an XRF reading of the brake rotor, and taking an XRF reading of an ingot made by casting pieces of the brake rotor, the process of casting does not significantly change the composition of the material.
